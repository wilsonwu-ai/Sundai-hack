# Catch risky agent steps before money moves

Thirty years of sequence-anomaly research support Sundai Bank's design as a credible hack-day baseline, on the condition that the team treats the model's blind spots as requirements rather than footnotes. Short-window models have flagged abnormal behaviour since Forrest et al. defined normal "by short-range correlations in a process' system calls" in 1996 ([Forrest et al. 1996](https://www.cs.unm.edu/~forrest/publications/ieee-sp-96-unix.pdf)). A 1999 comparison found hidden Markov models (HMMs) most accurate on average yet concluded that "weaker methods than HMMs are likely sufficient" ([Warrender et al. 1999](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/1418/1/DetectingIntrusions.pdf)), and DeepLog's 2017 LSTM beat an order-1 n-gram baseline by a modest margin, **833 versus 1,360 false positives and 619 versus 739 false negatives** on HDFS logs ([Du et al. 2017](https://acmccs.github.io/papers/p1285-duA.pdf)). The same sources mark the limits: an order-k model cannot see past k steps, longer windows starve for data, and a 2026 agent-trace paper warns that "adversarial traces preserving activity bigram statistics can replay" ([Cho et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23670)). An agent hijacked along a familiar path will therefore score as normal unless severity weights, rolling aggregates and hard rules catch it. On timing, regulators agree that the valuable moment is before execution: FinCEN calls catching fraudulent instructions "before payments are issued" essential ([FinCEN 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/2019-07/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)), Nacha concedes that pre-processing monitoring "provides the greatest opportunity" while declining to require it ([Nacha](https://www.nacha.org/rules/risk-management-topics-fraud-monitoring-phase-1)), and UK payment providers may hold a suspect payment until the end of the fourth business day ([SI 2024/1013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2024/1013/made)). OpenTelemetry's GenAI conventions already define the span shapes the team needs, but they remain at Development status with no tagged release. No detection result in this note has been validated on banking agents, and surprise times severity has no direct precedent, so the team's own evaluation is the evidence that will count.

## Thirty years of detectors favour short windows over clever models

Forrest et al. (1996) started the lineage with a deliberately simple detector: slide a fixed window over a program's system calls, store every window seen in training, and flag any window never seen before. The authors chose to "monitor only the presence or absence of patterns, not their relative frequency" and reported that "short sequences of system calls in running processes generate a stable signature for normal behavior" ([Forrest et al. 1996](https://www.cs.unm.edu/~forrest/publications/ieee-sp-96-unix.pdf)). The profiles were small. For sendmail at window size 6, **112 artificial messages producing over 1.5 million system calls yielded a database of roughly 1,500 entries**, and other common processes tested against it showed roughly 5 to 32 percent abnormal sequences, so programs could be told apart by their sequences alone. The paper already pointed to the Markov step, noting "we could represent the possible sequences of legal system calls as a Markov chain and define a criterion based on expected frequencies", and it named the tension every risk team inherits: "Too narrow a definition will result in many false positives, while too broad a definition of self will be tolerant of some unacceptable activities (false negatives)."

Warrender, Forrest and Pearlmutter (1999) ran the comparison that still frames the field. They tested simple enumeration of fixed-length sequences (stide, at lengths six and ten), a variant that also flags rare sequences (t-stide), RIPPER rule induction and HMMs across several program datasets ([Warrender et al. 1999](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/1418/1/DetectingIntrusions.pdf)). HMMs "gave the best accuracy on average, although at high computational costs", yet "the much simpler sequence time-delay embedding method compared favorably with HMMs." Three side results matter more to Sundai Bank than the winner. First, t-stide, which counted any sequence making up under 0.001% of training data as a mismatch, "consistently performs below the other methods". **Rarity is a poor stand-in for risk**, and that is the clearest argument in this literature for multiplying surprise by an independent severity weight. Second, the data mattered more than the method ("Results across modeling methods for a particular data set are more similar than results for the same method across different data sets"), and the authors concluded that "the choice of data stream (short sequences of system calls) is a more important decision than the particular method of analysis." For an agent fleet the data stream is the action alphabet, so deciding whether a token is a bare tool name or a tool plus a coarse argument class (new versus known payee, amount band) is the highest-leverage modelling choice. Third, two operating rules transfer directly. Alarms aggregated mismatches over a "locality frame" of recent steps instead of firing on one, and baseline collection stopped on a growth curve: "Once the rate of encountering new sequences drops below some preset value, we say we have enough data with which to build our model of normal." The authors were candid that their comparisons "are suggestive, rather than tests of statistical significance."

Banking borrowed the same move. Srivastava, Kundu, Sural and Majumdar (IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing, January 2008) "model the sequence of operations in credit card transaction processing using a hidden Markov model (HMM)", where "An HMM is initially trained with the normal behavior of a cardholder" and "If an incoming credit card transaction is not accepted by the trained HMM with sufficiently high probability, it is considered to be fraudulent" ([Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/TDSC.2007.70228); [abstract via Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1109/TDSC.2007.70228?fields=title,abstract,year,venue)). Scoring an event by the probability a per-entity model assigns it is the surprise half of Sundai Bank's score, and the per-cardholder baseline translates into a per-agent baseline. **Unverified:** only the abstract was checked against raw text. The paper's experimental results and its choice of observation symbols (secondary sources describe amount bands) were not read in the primary source.

DeepLog (CCS 2017) is the cleanest test of whether long memory pays. It trained an LSTM on normal log-key sequences alone, since it "only requires normal data to train", using under 1% of normal HDFS sessions (4,855) for training. It accepted each actual next key as normal when it fell among the g most probable predictions, with defaults of g = 9 and a history window of h = 10 ([Du et al. 2017](https://acmccs.github.io/papers/p1285-duA.pdf)). Against 553,366 normal and 15,200 abnormal test sessions drawn from a vocabulary of 29 log keys, its Table 4 reads as follows.

| Method on HDFS logs (Du et al. 2017, Table 4) | False positives | False negatives |
|---|---|---|
| PCA | 277 | 5,400 |
| Invariant mining | 2,122 | 1,217 |
| TF-IDF | 95,833 | 1,256 |
| N-gram baseline (N = 1) | 1,360 | 739 |
| DeepLog (LSTM) | 833 | 619 |

DeepLog "achieved the best overall performance, with an F-measure of 96%", yet **the n-gram baseline carried only about 1.6 times DeepLog's false positives and 1.2 times its false negatives, roughly 0.25% versus 0.15% of normal test sessions falsely flagged** (ratios and rates computed from Tables 3 and 4). That is a modest price for a model with no training loop. The top-g rule is worth borrowing on its own merits: because it is rank-based, a state with several legitimate next actions (check balance, verify payee, initiate transfer) is not penalised the way a raw surprise threshold penalises every branch.

## Every step of memory multiplies the contexts a model must learn

DeepLog's description of its baseline states the Markov assumption plainly: "The intuition is that a particular word in a sequence is only influenced by its recent predecessors rather than the entire history" ([Du et al. 2017](https://acmccs.github.io/papers/p1285-duA.pdf)). For an order-k model, anything more than k steps back does not exist, and that matters because the banking patterns regulators care about are often long-range. FinCEN's red flags include payments "immediately following a successful payment to an account not previously used" ([FinCEN 2016](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2016-09-09/FIN-2016-A003.pdf)). Its definition of structuring, "the breaking up of transactions for the purpose of evading the Bank Secrecy Act reporting and recordkeeping requirements", covers activity spread across "multiple transactions during one day or over a period of several days or more" ([FinCEN Ruling 2005-6](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/administrative_ruling/fincenruling2005-6.pdf)). Structuring is a cash-reporting concept, so it serves as precedent for threshold-aware aggregation rather than as a rule that applies to agents. The shape is the same, though: a large intent split into individually unremarkable steps that a short window sees one at a time.

Lengthening the window is the obvious fix and an expensive one, because **the number of possible contexts grows as |A|^k** for an alphabet of |A| actions. With an illustrative alphabet of 30 action types, an order-3 model has 27,000 possible contexts and an order-5 model 24.3 million, so most contexts in a hack-day log will never appear in training (arithmetic only; the team's real alphabet will differ). Warrender et al. hit exactly this wall. Their length-ten training set was much larger because "there are many more possible sequences of length ten than of length six", and stide and RIPPER did "slightly better for sequence length six, despite training on less data, possibly because the smaller sequences allow better generalization", while HMMs "do not depend on the sequence length" ([Warrender et al. 1999](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/1418/1/DetectingIntrusions.pdf)). DeepLog found the same pattern. Its n-gram baseline was run at N = 1 "since this shows the best performance for the N-gram method", and "its performance drops dramatically as history window becomes longer. In contrast, LSTM-based approach is more stable" ([Du et al. 2017](https://acmccs.github.io/papers/p1285-duA.pdf)). Even the LSTM gains from extra history only "until it reaches a point where keys that are far back in history do not contribute".

Language modelling worked out the sparsity half of this problem decades ago. Jurafsky and Martin note that "any finite training corpus will be missing some perfectly acceptable English word sequences", so unseen n-grams mean "we are underestimating the probability of word sequences that might occur" ([Jurafsky and Martin, ch. 3](https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/3.pdf)). The standard remedies are interpolation, which mixes order-3, order-2 and order-1 estimates, and backoff, which drops to a shorter context when the longer one has no counts. Modified Interpolated Kneser-Ney became "the standard baseline for n-gram language modeling around the turn of the century", while the cheap "stupid backoff" variant "gives up the idea of trying to make the language model a true probability distribution". For Sundai Bank that points to **an interpolated order-1 to order-3 model per agent, with a probability floor for never-seen actions**. Scoring an unseen context as maximal surprise turns sparsity itself into false pauses, which is the t-stide failure in a new form. No source found tests these smoothing methods on small tool-call alphabets, so the order should be chosen on held-out traces rather than assumed.

Agent traces look unusually friendly to small Markov models. Cho et al. (August 2026) compiled each of twelve public agent-trace corpora into one finite-state machine (FSM) of 7 to 43 states that replayed held-out data at 0.997 fitness or better. They found that "FSM state conditioning improves cross-entropy by 0.155 bits (21%) over identical methods without state" ([Cho et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23670)), and they credit the environment: "bounded LLM-agent alphabets make the resulting compact deterministic finite automaton (DFA) both small and statistically informative", and "Behavioral topology thus appears shaped more by the deployment harness than by the LLM". For Sundai Bank, that means the eight agents' grammars are set largely by the bank's own tool design. The paper scores each step by its surprise, `-log2 P(a_t | q_t)` under the FSM state, reports that "Failure traces show higher surprise under the FSM's transition distribution", and argues that these features "give a continuous per-step risk score, whereas ProbGuard's PCTL-thresholded reachability is binary at deployment". It also states the blind spot formally: "The FSM accepts the directly-follows closure of the observed traces, not the agent's generating language; adversarial traces preserving activity bigram statistics can replay". **A prompt-injected agent that walks a familiar sequence of tools toward an unfamiliar payee is invisible to surprise and visible only to severity and rules.**

Other agent-trace detectors exist, but none yet clears the bar a bank would want. TraceAegis (October 2025) learns execution units and behavioural rules from traces because hand-written rules leave "gaps that result in false negatives". It reports F1 of 0.93 and 0.96 on its own benchmark of 1,300 benign and 300 abnormal behaviours in each of two scenarios, healthcare and corporate procurement ([Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11203)). Trajectory Guard (January 2026, a single independent author) reports F1 of 0.88 to 0.94 on balanced synthetic perturbation sets and 32 ms inference latency, against embedding baselines "no higher than 0.69" ([Advani 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.00516v1)). Both are preprints evaluated on author-built or synthetic benchmarks, with no adaptive attacker and no banking data, and Cho et al., though tested on public datasets, target failure prediction rather than security. The research team found no paper that scores tool calls by an LLM's next-action likelihood. The pointer worth keeping is Cho et al.'s Corollary 7: when success and failure traces come from FSM-structured mixtures, the per-trace difference in surprise between a failure model and a success model is a Neyman-Pearson-optimal statistic for telling them apart, up to an error that shrinks as each state accumulates visits. If the team scripts attack traces for its demo, training a second Markov model on them turns plain surprise into a log-likelihood ratio, which is the principled upgrade path.

## Card systems score history after approval, agents can score it before

The card-fraud literature draws the online and offline line precisely. Dal Pozzolo et al. (IEEE TNNLS 2017) describe a real-world fraud detection system "inspired by the one routinely used by our industrial partner". It has five layers: terminal, transaction-blocking rules, scoring rules, a data-driven model and investigators, where "Layers i) - iv) fully implement automatic controls, while the layer v) is the only one requiring human intervention" ([Dal Pozzolo et al. 2017](https://boracchi.faculty.polimi.it/docs/2017_FraudsTNNLS.pdf)). Only the first two layers act before approval, and they are deliberately shallow. Online terminal checks "have to be performed in real time (response has to be provided in a few milliseconds)", and blocking rules "use the few information available when the payment is requested, without analyzing historical records or cardholder profile." The history-aware layers then "assign a score to each authorized transaction", and "Classifiers are typically employed to analyze all the authorized transactions and alert the most suspicious ones." **The classic card stack, in other words, runs its history-aware models after the payment decision, because the latency budget leaves no room for them before it.** Sundai Bank's design inverts that order, and an agent loop can afford to: a step that already waits on a model call has time for a table lookup over the action history before a transfer tool fires.

The offline loop is slow and sparse by construction. Most transactions "cannot be verified by investigators for obvious time and cost constraints" and "remain unlabeled until customers discover and report frauds, or until a sufficient amount of time has elapsed such that non-disputed transactions are considered genuine". The authors call this "verification latency (only a small set of transactions are timely checked by investigators)" and study it on a stream of "more than 75 millions transactions, authorized over a time window of three years" ([Dal Pozzolo et al. 2017](https://boracchi.faculty.polimi.it/docs/2017_FraudsTNNLS.pdf)). Labels in any agent-risk system will behave the same way, which shapes how lead time must be measured.

Regulators agree that the pre-execution moment is where intervention pays. FinCEN's 2019 advisory says recovery succeeds more often when business email compromise wires are reported to law enforcement "within 24 hours", that such transfers "are often irrevocable", and that "Identifying fraudulent transaction payment instructions before payments are issued is therefore essential to preventing and reducing unauthorized transactions" ([FinCEN 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/2019-07/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). Nacha's 2026 ACH fraud-monitoring rule (Phase 1 effective March 20, 2026, Phase 2 June 19, 2026) concedes the point while declining to mandate it: "Although monitoring transactions prior to processing provides the greatest opportunity for detecting and preventing potential fraud, the rules do not require such monitoring to be performed prior to processing Entries" ([Nacha](https://www.nacha.org/rules/risk-management-topics-fraud-monitoring-phase-1)). The UK went furthest. The Payment Services (Amendment) Regulations 2024, in force since 30 October 2024, let a payer's provider delay a payment "for the purpose of contacting the payer or other relevant third party to make further enquiries in order to establish whether it should execute the payment order". The trigger is "reasonable grounds to suspect a payment order from a payer has been placed subsequent to fraud or dishonesty perpetrated by a person other than the payer", and the hold lasts "no longer than the end of the fourth business day following the time of receipt of the payment order" ([SI 2024/1013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2024/1013/made)). **That clause is a ready template for an agent pause: bounded in time, limited in purpose and triggered by a stated test.** Confirmation of Payee shows where else a gate belongs, since it checks the name when "Customers setting up a new payee" enter it ([PSR 2020](https://www.psr.org.uk/news-and-updates/latest-news/news/psr-confirms-widespread-implementation-of-name-checking-system-confirmation-of-payee/)). Sundai Bank should score the add-payee action, not only the transfer.

Latency budgets are the weakest strand of evidence. No card-network or payment-rail budget could be verified. Mastercard's reported sub-50-millisecond scoring claim for its generative-AI Decision Intelligence comes only from search summaries (its page returned HTTP 403 to a raw fetch) and is **unverified**. A US patent says an issuer "may be expected to provide a response within a timeout threshold (e.g., typically 5 to 10 seconds, etc.)", which is patent text rather than a network rule ([US12118555](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12118555)). Dal Pozzolo's "a few milliseconds" for terminal checks is the only primary-source figure in this evidence, and it describes the shallow layer. For an agent gate the budget is set by the agent loop itself, so latency bites mainly for LLM-based auditors such as LlamaFirewall's AlignmentCheck, which "utilizes few-shot prompting to audit an agent's reasoning in real-time" and "reasons over the entire execution trace" ([AlignmentCheck docs](https://meta-llama.github.io/PurpleLlama/LlamaFirewall/docs/documentation/scanners/alignment-check)).

## Lead time only means something against the first harmful step

Early detection has a formal home in the early classification of time series, whose goal is to "recognize the class of an incoming time series as quickly as possible without unduly compromising the accuracy of the prediction" ([Achenchabe et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.09945)). Achenchabe et al. frame it as a criterion that "takes into account both the cost of misclassification and the cost of delaying the decision", because "the longer the decision is delayed, the clearer is the likely outcome" while the cost of waiting keeps rising. Their bibliography traces the line to Xing, Pei and Yu's 2009 "Early prediction on time series: A nearest neighbor approach". The minimum prediction length concept usually credited to Xing et al. could not be read in primary text and is **unverified**. Fraud work measures something else, detection at investigator capacity, where "it is most appropriate to assess the number of detected fraudulent transactions (or cards) over the maximum number of transactions (or cards) that investigators can check" ([Dal Pozzolo et al. 2017](https://boracchi.faculty.polimi.it/docs/2017_FraudsTNNLS.pdf)). Neither field supplies a standard lead-time metric for payments, and the research team found no regulator definition of time to detect.

Combining the two gives Sundai Bank a defensible metric set. **Lead time is the number of steps and seconds between the first pause and the first harmful `execute_tool` call**, the money-moving step, measured against that step rather than against label arrival, because labels arrive late. It should always be reported beside the false-pause rate at the same threshold and, following the capacity idea, as precision at a fixed pause budget such as pauses per 1,000 actions. Cho et al. offer a sobering calibration point for prefix-based monitoring: their monitor "ranks failing SWE-agent runs above passing ones at the 25% checkpoint (rank-AUROC 0.66 vs. 0.5 for flag-everything) and triggers early stopping at 32% completion" ([Cho et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23670)). Early signal exists, but a quarter of the way into a trace it is weak, which is why the severity term and the gate's placement matter more than squeezing earlier detection out of surprise.

The most useful precursor features come from FinCEN's 2016 business email compromise red flags, which its 2019 advisory says "remain relevant" ([FinCEN 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/2019-07/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). They cover instructions to a known beneficiary whose "account information is different from what was previously used"; a beneficiary "with which the customer has no payment history or documented business relationship" receiving "an amount similar to or in excess of" historical payments; requests labelled urgent, secret or confidential; instructions from "a newly authorized person on the account"; and follow-on payments to a newly used account ([FinCEN 2016](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2016-09-09/FIN-2016-A003.pdf)). Each maps onto Sundai Bank's action log as a feature a short window will often miss: a payee-detail update earlier in the session followed by a transfer, a first-time payee combined with a high amount percentile, a second transfer to the same new payee. Nacha's monitoring guidance points at the same family, naming "transactional velocity, anomalies (e.g., SEC Code mismatch with account type), and account characteristics (e.g., age of account, average balance, etc.)" ([Nacha](https://www.nacha.org/rules/risk-management-topics-fraud-monitoring-phase-1)). **These belong in the severity term or in hard rules, not in a longer Markov window.**

Evaluation is where hack-day numbers most often mislead. Dal Pozzolo et al. argue that most published fraud algorithms rest on "assumptions that hardly hold in a real-world Fraud Detection System (FDS)", chiefly the timing of labels and the choice of metrics. They stress "class imbalance (genuine transactions far outnumber frauds)" and "concept drift (customers habits evolve and fraudsters change their strategies over time)" ([Dal Pozzolo et al. 2017](https://boracchi.faculty.polimi.it/docs/2017_FraudsTNNLS.pdf)). The practical rules follow: split traces by time, never at random; compute every feature only from events before the decision point; and count lead time only for harmful events whose precursors fall wholly inside the test period. Synthetic data needs extra care. The 2016 PaySim simulator paper states that "The injection of malicious fraud behaviour and the application of different fraud detection methods are outside the scope of this paper" ([Lopez-Rojas et al. 2016](https://msc-les.org/proceedings/emss/2016/EMSS2016_249.pdf)), and a Kaggle note warning that the public PaySim dataset's balance columns leak the fraud label is **unverified**. Any lead-time figure measured on scripted or simulated attacks is a pipeline check, not evidence of detection power.

## OpenTelemetry gives agent traces a shared shape, not yet a stable one

The GenAI conventions now live in their own repository, open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai (Apache-2.0, created 5 May 2026, last push 7 November 2026), and the old agent-spans page in the core repository reads "This page has moved and is no longer maintained in this repository" ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai); [old location](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-agent-spans.md)). Stability is the headline caveat. **The GenAI index, agent-spans and execute-tool pages are all marked Development**, the changelog holds only an "Unreleased" heading, and the README's schema URL section reads "TODO" ([GenAI index](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/README.md); [CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/CHANGELOG.md); [README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/README.md)). Names have already shifted once: Microsoft Agent Framework documents that "Latest-mode span attributes emit gen_ai.provider.name; v1.36 span attributes and message events emit gen_ai.system" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/agent-framework/agents/observability)). The PRD should pin a commit of the conventions and expect renames.

The agent model itself is compact. The registry's well-known `gen_ai.operation.name` values include `create_agent`, `invoke_agent`, `invoke_workflow`, `invoke_runner`, `plan`, `execute_tool` and `chat` ([attribute registry](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/registry/attributes/gen-ai.md)). The in-process `invoke_agent` span "Describes an in-process GenAI agent, workflow, or framework entrypoint invocation", takes its name from the operation and agent name, and has kind INTERNAL. The spec uses `invoke_agent` "when an LLM drives the control flow through tool calls" and `invoke_workflow` "when the application defines the control flow, for example LangGraph `StateGraph`" ([agent spans](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-agent-spans.md)). Each tool call becomes an INTERNAL span named `execute_tool {gen_ai.tool.name}`, in which `gen_ai.tool.name` is Required, `gen_ai.tool.call.id` Conditionally Required, `gen_ai.tool.type` Recommended, and `gen_ai.tool.call.arguments` and `gen_ai.tool.call.result` Opt-In ([execute tool](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/execute-tool.md)). **For the risk team, the ordered `execute_tool` spans under each agent invocation are the action sequence the Markov model reads.**

The attribute rules fit a fixed fleet well. `gen_ai.agent.name` suits the eight agents exactly, since "This attribute MUST have low cardinality" and "When no meaningful, low-cardinality name is available, this attribute MUST NOT be captured by default" ([attribute registry](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/registry/attributes/gen-ai.md)). `gen_ai.agent.id` is aimed at provider-hosted agents, with the warning "It's NOT RECOMMENDED to record in-memory agent instance ids on this attribute due to their transient nature" ([agent spans](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-agent-spans.md)). Sessions map to `gen_ai.conversation.id`, "The unique identifier for a conversation (session, thread), used to store and correlate messages within this conversation", which instrumentations can push down to child spans: "Instrumentations MAY propagate context containing attributes listed below to nested instrumentations within the same process" ([GenAI index](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/README.md)). The spec also says key attributes, agent name and operation name among them, "SHOULD be provided at span creation time" so that samplers can use them.

Content capture is off unless someone turns it on. "Capturing model instructions, prompts, completions, retrieval queries, prompt template variables, and memory records is gated by an explicit user opt-in", and a switch exposed as an environment variable "MUST be named `OTEL_INSTRUMENTATION_GENAI_CAPTURE_CONTENT`", whose default, `NO_CONTENT`, means "Do not capture content on spans or events" ([GenAI spans](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-spans.md)). Tool arguments and results carry the warning "This attribute may contain sensitive information" ([execute tool](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/execute-tool.md)). For a bank the default should stay off. The risk model needs coarse, hashed or banded fields, and no GenAI attribute covers amount, payee novelty, risk score or pause decision, so those belong under a custom prefix such as `bank.*`. That prefix is a team convention drawn from general OpenTelemetry practice; no GenAI-specific rule on custom namespaces was found. The mapping below follows from the spec text above.

| Sundai Bank event field | OpenTelemetry placement | What the spec says |
|---|---|---|
| Session identifier | Trace ID plus `gen_ai.conversation.id` | Conditionally Required on `invoke_agent` when available |
| Agent (one of eight) | `gen_ai.agent.name` | MUST have low cardinality |
| Task run by an agent | `invoke_agent` span, kind INTERNAL | Use `invoke_workflow` if the application, not the LLM, drives control flow |
| Tool call (the Markov token) | `execute_tool {gen_ai.tool.name}` span | `gen_ai.tool.name` Required |
| Tool call identifier | `gen_ai.tool.call.id` | Conditionally Required |
| Arguments and results | `gen_ai.tool.call.arguments`, `gen_ai.tool.call.result` | Opt-In and flagged sensitive; prefer hashes or bands |
| Amount band, payee novelty, surprise, severity, pause decision | Custom prefix such as `bank.*` | No GenAI attribute exists (team convention, not spec) |
| Failure | `error.type` | Conditionally Required when the operation ends in an error |

Transport is stable even where the conventions are not. OTLP is "Stable for the trace, metric and log signals"; "The default network port for OTLP/HTTP is 4318", and "The default URL path for requests that carry trace data is /v1/traces" ([OTLP spec](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otlp/)). One trap catches hand-rolled JSONL exporters: "The traceId and spanId byte arrays are represented as case-insensitive hex-encoded strings; they are not base64-encoded as is defined in the standard Protobuf JSON Mapping." The research team reproduced the trap locally with opentelemetry-sdk 1.45.2, where the generic protobuf-to-JSON path wrote base64 trace IDs, so **the JSONL writer must hex-encode IDs to stay joinable with the spans**. In Python, "The ConsoleSpanExporter and ConsoleMetricExporter are included in the opentelemetry-sdk package", and the OTLP/HTTP exporter ships separately as opentelemetry-exporter-otlp-proto-http, both at version 1.45.2 on PyPI ([Python exporters](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/languages/python/exporters/); [PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/opentelemetry-exporter-otlp-proto-http/json)). The sturdiest pattern is to build each event once as a dictionary and write it to both sinks, so the JSONL log and the spans cannot disagree.

Framework support is uneven, which matters only if the team swaps its custom agent loop for a framework. Microsoft Agent Framework "emits traces, logs, and metrics according to the OpenTelemetry GenAI Semantic Conventions", but any set value of `OTEL_SEMCONV_STABILITY_OPT_IN` that omits the `gen_ai_latest_experimental` token "selects the v1.36 conventions" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/agent-framework/agents/observability)). Google's ADK "implements the OpenTelemetry (OTel) Semantic Conventions for GenAI" and lists `invoke_agent`, `invoke_workflow`, `execute_tool` and `generate_content` spans ([ADK docs](https://adk.dev/observability/traces/)), and Pydantic AI's instrumentation docs point to the same conventions ([Pydantic AI](https://pydantic.dev/docs/ai/capabilities/instrumentation/)). LangChain and LangGraph export OpenTelemetry through LangSmith's `LANGSMITH_OTEL_ENABLED` switch, and LangSmith's attribute mapping mixes legacy `gen_ai.prompt.{n}` fields with the newer `gen_ai.input.messages` ([LangSmith](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/trace-with-opentelemetry)). The OpenAI Agents SDK by default "exports the spans and traces to the OpenAI backend" and reaches OpenTelemetry through external tracing processors ([OpenAI Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/tracing/)). The Claude Agent SDK exports OTLP "Spans for each interaction, model request, tool call, and hook (beta)" under `claude_code.*` names, with no `gen_ai` attribute anywhere on its documentation page ([Claude Agent SDK](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/observability)).

Invariant's rule syntax shows how close guardrails and the Markov model already are. A workable hack-day topology follows from the table: agents export OTLP/HTTP to a local OpenTelemetry Collector on 4318, which fans out to Jaeger for people and to a file or custom consumer for the risk model, while the JSONL log remains the system of record. ## Conclusion

The evidence moves the risk in Sundai Bank's plan away from the model and onto three design decisions the literature keeps returning to. The alphabet comes first: Warrender et al. ranked the data stream above the method, and Cho et al. found agent behaviour shaped by the harness, so the team's own tool design largely determines how learnable its agents are. The gate's position comes second. The card industry runs history-aware models after approval because of latency, while an agent loop can afford them before the transfer tool fires, which is exactly the point regulators identify as most valuable. The complements come third, because surprise cannot see mimicry or split transfers. Since surprise times severity has no precedent in anything reviewed, the honest demo-day claim is a baseline with named blind spots plus explicit rules, and the principled next step is a log-likelihood ratio against a second model trained on scripted attacks.

The trace layer is the part most likely to outlive the hack. Emitting the spec's `invoke_agent` and `execute_tool` shapes now lets one span stream feed Jaeger for people, the Markov scorer for the risk team and any guardrail added later. Recording every human decision on a paused step as a span attribute turns the pause queue into a label factory, producing at decision time the labels that card investigators wait days for and supplying the training data for that likelihood-ratio upgrade. Because the conventions are still in Development and have already renamed a core attribute, a pinned commit and a `bank.*` namespace are what keep the schema usable once the conventions settle.
